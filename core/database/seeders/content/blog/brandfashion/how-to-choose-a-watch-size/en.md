The same watch can look elegant on one wrist and oversized on another. Size is the single biggest reason a watch feels "right" or never quite does — more than the brand, more than the colour. The good news: you do not need to try a hundred watches. Three measurements and a few rules of thumb get you very close.

## First, measure your wrist

Wrap a soft tape measure — or a strip of paper you then measure against a ruler — around your wrist, just above the wrist bone, where a watch sits. Note the number in centimetres. Measure the wrist you wear your watch on, at the end of the day, when it is at its largest.

Write it down. You will use it to choose the case size, and later, if you buy a watch on a bracelet, to have it sized.

## The three numbers on a product page

- **Case diameter.** The width of the case, in millimetres, usually without the crown. This is the number most people mean by "size".
- **Thickness.** How tall the case stands off the wrist. A thick watch looks bigger than its diameter suggests and can catch on a shirt cuff.
- **Lug-to-lug.** The distance between the tips of the lugs, where the strap attaches. If it is wider than your wrist, the lugs hang over the edge — the clearest sign a watch is too big.

## Rules of thumb for the case size

These are a starting point, not a law — the shape of the case and the dial change the picture.

- **A smaller wrist** (roughly up to 16 cm) is usually flattered by a case of around 34 to 38 mm.
- **A medium wrist** (about 16 to 18 cm) carries 38 to 42 mm comfortably.
- **A larger wrist** (18 cm and above) can wear 42 mm and up.

Women's watches are often smaller, but there is no rule: many women wear larger watches as a statement, and many men prefer a small, classic case.

## What makes a watch look bigger or smaller

Two watches with the same diameter can look very different:

- **The bezel.** A thin bezel and a wide dial look bigger; a thick bezel makes the dial look smaller.
- **The dial colour.** Light dials tend to look larger than dark ones.
- **The shape.** A square or rectangular case wears differently from a round one of the same width.
- **The strap.** A wide bracelet makes a watch look heavier; a slim leather strap makes it look lighter.

## Matching size to style

- **Dress watches** are traditionally smaller and slimmer, so they slip under a shirt cuff. See our [dress watches](/subtypes/dress).
- **Sports watches and chronographs** are usually larger and thicker, because of the bezel, the pushers and the sub-dials. See our [sports watches](/subtypes/sport).

If you want one watch for everything, a medium size in a classic shape is the safest choice.

## The bracelet fit

A watch that is the right size still needs the right fit:

- It should sit just above the wrist bone without sliding down over the hand.
- You should be able to slide one finger under the bracelet or strap.
- It should not leave a mark when you take it off.

A steel bracelet is sized by removing or adding links, and **bracelet sizing is free within the warranty**. A leather strap simply has several holes — choose the one where it sits flat and comfortable.

## Buying for someone else

Choosing a size for a gift is easier than it sounds. If the person already wears a watch, look at its case size — on its caseback, its box or its product page — and choose something close to it. If they do not, think about their wrist compared with yours: noticeably slimmer, about the same, or broader. When you are unsure, the safest choice is a classic medium case on a steel bracelet, because the bracelet can be sized after the gift is opened and a medium case suits most wrists. Keep the seals closed until the gift is opened, so the exchange stays possible.

## Before you order, a quick check

1. Measure your wrist and note it.
2. Compare the case diameter with the guide above.
3. Check the thickness and, if listed, the lug-to-lug.
4. Think about where you will wear it: under a cuff, or out in the open.
5. Look at a watch you already own and like — its size is your best reference.
