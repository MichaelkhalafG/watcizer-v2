A chronograph is the watch with the extra buttons and the small dials — the one that looks as if it belongs on a racing driver's or a pilot's wrist. It is also one of the most practical complications a watch can have. If you are choosing one, it helps to know what each part does and which features you will actually use.

## What a chronograph is

A chronograph is a watch with a stopwatch built in. It tells the time like any other watch, and it can also measure how long something takes — a lap, a meeting, a parking meter, the eggs on the stove — and show it on the dial.

The word is often confused with **chronometer**. A chronometer is a watch certified for its accuracy (in Switzerland by the official testing institute, COSC); a chronograph is a watch with a stopwatch function. A watch can be one, the other, both or neither.

## The pushers: start, stop, reset

On most chronographs, two buttons sit beside the crown:

- **The top pusher (near 2 o'clock)** starts the stopwatch, and stops it. Press it again to restart from where it stopped.
- **The bottom pusher (near 4 o'clock)** resets it to zero — once it has been stopped.

That is all most people ever need. Unless your watch's booklet says it can, do not press the pushers under water: they are a way in for water while they are being pressed.

## The sub-dials: reading what you measured

The small dials on a chronograph each have a job, and the layout differs from model to model:

- **The large central seconds hand**, on most chronographs, is the stopwatch's seconds. It stays still until you start the chronograph, then sweeps around the dial.
- **A minutes counter** — often marked to 30 or 60 — counts the minutes the stopwatch has been running.
- **An hours counter**, on many models, counts the hours, often up to 12.
- **A small running-seconds dial** shows that the watch itself is running, since the big seconds hand is reserved for the stopwatch.

So to read a measured time, add up the hours, the minutes and the seconds shown by the counters and the central hand. The booklet in the box shows your model's layout.

## The tachymeter: speed from a time

Many chronographs carry a scale on the bezel or around the dial marked **tachymeter**. It turns an elapsed time into a speed, over a known distance.

Start the chronograph as you pass a marker — say, a kilometre post — and stop it at the next. The number the seconds hand points to on the tachymeter scale is your average speed in kilometres per hour. The scale is simply 3,600 divided by the seconds: a kilometre covered in 35 seconds reads about 103. It works with any unit of distance, as long as you use the same one, and it can count anything that happens at a steady rate — units produced per hour, for example.

You may never time a car between two posts. But the tachymeter is part of the chronograph's look, and many owners choose it for exactly that.

## Quartz or automatic?

Chronographs come with both kinds of movement:

- **A quartz chronograph** is accurate, needs no winding, and is usually slimmer and lighter. Its battery needs changing every few years — free at Watchizer within the warranty. - **An automatic chronograph** is mechanical, wound by the movement of your wrist, and a fine piece of engineering in its own right: the first ones appeared only in 1969. It is usually thicker and asks to be worn regularly.

Neither is better in itself. The question is which suits how you will live with the watch.

## Choosing one that suits you

- **Size.** Chronographs tend to run large, because the sub-dials need space. Look at the case diameter on the product page and compare it with a watch you already wear; on a slimmer wrist, the thickness matters as much as the width.
- **Legibility.** Can you read the hands and the sub-dials at a glance? Contrast — light hands on a dark dial, or the reverse — helps more than any feature.
- **The bracelet or strap.** A steel bracelet for everyday and water; leather for a dressier look. A chronograph on a bracelet carries its weight well.
- **Water resistance.** Check the rating against your day. A chronograph for the pool should be rated for swimming, and the pushers stay untouched under water.
- **Features you will use.** A simple two-pusher chronograph does everything most people need. Extras such as a flyback (a reset-and-restart in one press) are lovely to have, but only worth paying for if you will use them.
