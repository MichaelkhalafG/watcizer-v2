A watch is one of the few things you buy that can last decades, and it is also one of the most copied products in the world. The good news is that an original watch carries its own proof, and once you know where to look, most of it takes a minute to check. This guide walks through the evidence that matters, in the order we check it ourselves at Watchizer, where we have been selling watches since 2013.

## Start with the serial number and its stamps

Every genuine watch from an established brand carries a serial number, and usually a reference (model) number as well. Where you find them depends on the brand: on the caseback, between the lugs, on the inner side of the case, or on the movement itself. What matters is not only that a number exists, but that it is **stamped or engraved cleanly**, at the depth and in the typeface the brand uses.

Look closely at the engraving. On an original, the characters are sharp, evenly spaced and aligned. Printed numbers, shallow etching that looks like it could be rubbed off, uneven spacing or a font that does not match the rest of the caseback markings are all reasons to stop and ask questions.

Then compare. The serial and reference number on the watch should match the numbers on its paperwork — the warranty card, and where the brand provides one, the tag or booklet that came with it. A watch whose numbers do not appear anywhere in its documents, or whose card names a different model, is not a watch to buy.

## The brand box and the catalogue

An original watch arrives in the brand's own presentation box, with the brand's catalogue or instruction booklet. The box is not a decoration: its materials, the quality of the printing and the fit of the cushion are part of what the brand controls, and the booklet describes the functions of that model's movement.

A box on its own proves nothing — boxes can be bought separately — but a box that does not match the watch inside it, a booklet for a different movement, or a missing booklet altogether are signals. When everything belongs together, the box, the booklet, the card and the watch tell the same story.

## Check the details a copy gets wrong

Once the paperwork is in order, look at the watch itself:

- **The dial.** Printing should be crisp at every size, including the smallest text on a sub-dial or a tachymeter scale. Logos should be applied or printed exactly as on the brand's own photographs.
- **The hands and markers.** They should be aligned with the indexes, cleanly finished, and the seconds hand should land on the markers.
- **The crown and pushers.** On an original they move with a precise, consistent feel. A crown that wobbles, or pushers that feel loose, are worth a second look.
- **The bracelet and clasp.** Links should be even, the clasp should close firmly, and the engraving on the clasp should be as clean as the engraving on the case.
- **The weight.** Steel and solid metals feel substantial. A watch that feels hollow or unexpectedly light for its size deserves questions.

None of these alone is proof, which is why the serial number, the box and the papers come first. But together they let you see quickly whether the watch in front of you is what it claims to be.

## A short checklist

1. Find the serial and reference numbers, and check that they are cleanly engraved.
2. Make sure the numbers match the warranty card and the paperwork.
3. Check that the box and the booklet belong to the brand and to that model.
4. Ask for a warranty card with a stated length, and a sales invoice.
5. Look at the dial, hands, crown, pushers and bracelet for clean, precise finishing.
