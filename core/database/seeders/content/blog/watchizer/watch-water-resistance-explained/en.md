Turn almost any watch over and you will find it on the caseback: "Water Resistant", followed by a number — 30 m, 50 m, 100 m, sometimes 3 ATM or 10 BAR. It is one of the most misread lines on a watch. It does not tell you how deep you can swim, and reading it that way is the most common reason a watch meets water it was never made for. Here is what the mark actually means, and how to choose a watch for the life you will give it.

## The number is a test pressure, not a depth

The figure on the caseback records the pressure a sample of newly made watches withstood in a still-water test. It can be written as a pressure (3 ATM, 5 BAR) or, more often, as the depth of water that would produce that pressure (30 m, 50 m). The two say the same thing: 10 metres of water is roughly one atmosphere.

What the test does not include is movement. When you swim, dive into a pool or stand under a strong shower, water hits the watch with more force than still water at the same depth, the temperature changes, and soap and chlorine work on the seals. So a watch marked 30 m is not a watch for swimming at 30 metres — or for swimming at all.

The word "waterproof" is not used on watches for the same reason: the ISO standard for water-resistant watches does not allow it, and today's version of that standard, ISO 22810, sets the tests a watch sold as water resistant must pass. A watch resists water up to a point; none is proof against it for ever.

## What each mark is usually good for

Manufacturers' guidance varies, so the product page and the booklet in the box are the final word for your watch. As a general guide:

- **30 m / 3 ATM** — everyday wear. Rain, splashes and washing your hands are fine. Not for showering, swimming or bathing.
- **50 m / 5 ATM** — everyday wear plus a quick shower and light, shallow swimming. Not for diving.
- **100 m / 10 ATM** — swimming, snorkelling and most surface water sports. Not for scuba diving.
- **200 m / 20 ATM** — serious water sports and skin diving.
- **"Diver's" with a depth (for example DIVER'S 200 M)** — a diving watch tested to its own standard, ISO 6425. Each watch is tested individually, including at 125% of its rated pressure, and it is built for repeated use under water.

If you swim regularly, 100 metres is the practical minimum. If you dive, look for a watch marked as a diver's watch, not just a high number. Our [diver's watches](/subtypes/diver) and [sports watches](/subtypes/sport) are the place to start.

## The parts that keep water out

Water resistance is not a property of the case alone. It depends on a few small parts working together:

- **The gaskets.** Rubber seals at the caseback, the crystal and the crown. They age, dry out and harden over time, and they are what fails first.
- **The crown.** The most common way in for water is a crown left pulled out. Some watches have a screw-down crown, which locks against the case — screw it fully home before the watch goes near water.
- **The pushers.** On a chronograph, the buttons that start and stop the stopwatch. Unless the watch says otherwise, do not press them under water.
- **The caseback.** Screwed or pressed into place, and sealed. Every time a back is opened — for a battery change, for instance — the seal has to be seated again properly.

## How to keep a watch water resistant

A watch that left the factory water resistant does not stay that way on its own. A few habits make the difference:

- **Check the crown before every swim or shower.** Pushed in, or screwed down if it screws.
- **Rinse after the sea or a pool.** Salt and chlorine attack the gaskets; a rinse in fresh water and a dry with a soft cloth removes them.
- **Avoid hot water and sudden temperature changes.** Saunas, hot tubs and a jump from the sun into cold water make the seals expand and contract.
- **Keep a leather strap dry.** Even on a water-resistant watch, leather does not like water. For swimming, a steel bracelet or a rubber strap is the better choice.
- **Have the seals checked.** Gaskets age whether the watch gets wet or not. A battery change is the right moment to have them looked at.
- **Act quickly on condensation.** Mist under the crystal means moisture got in. Stop wearing the watch in water and bring it to be checked before the movement suffers.

## When water damage is — and is not — covered

Every watch we sell comes with a warranty of up to 3 years, depending on the watch, covering the movement against manufacturing defects. Water that enters because a crown was left out, or because a 30-metre watch went swimming, is not a manufacturing defect. That is one more reason to choose a watch whose rating matches how you will wear it, and to treat the crown with care.

## Choosing with the water in mind

Before you buy, ask yourself one question: where will this watch get wet? If the answer is "in the rain and at the sink", any water-resistant watch will do. If it is "in the pool every morning", start at 100 metres and choose a bracelet or rubber strap. If it is "under the sea", choose a diver's watch.
