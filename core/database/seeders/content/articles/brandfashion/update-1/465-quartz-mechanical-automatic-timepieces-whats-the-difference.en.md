Every analogue watch is driven by one of three kinds of movement. A quartz movement uses a battery and a vibrating quartz crystal; a mechanical movement uses a wound spring, a balance wheel and a train of gears; and an automatic movement is a mechanical one with a rotor that winds the spring as you move. This guide opens the case back, figuratively, and explains how each one actually works, part by part, so you understand what you are buying and why each kind of watch behaves as it does. We carry {{count family=watch}} watches at Brand Fashion, and most of them are quartz.

## Key points

- A quartz watch counts the vibrations of a quartz crystal, 32,768 per second, and moves the hands with a tiny stepping motor.
- A mechanical watch stores energy in a mainspring and releases it through an escapement, tick by tick, regulated by a balance wheel.
- An automatic watch adds a weighted rotor that winds the mainspring as your wrist moves.
- Jewels are synthetic rubies used as bearings to reduce friction in mechanical movements; they add no value as gems.
- Quartz is the most accurate; mechanical watches need regular wear or winding and a service every few years.

## The three families at a glance

Before looking inside, it helps to know how to recognise each family from the outside. A quartz watch's seconds hand moves in one step every second. A mechanical or automatic watch's seconds hand moves in many small steps each second and appears to sweep. A hand-wound mechanical watch has to be wound by turning the crown, usually once a day; an automatic winds itself as long as you wear it.

The product page tells you which one you are buying. At Brand Fashion the movement is listed with each watch, along with the case size, the water resistance and the warranty. If a description mentions a skeleton or open-heart dial, the watch is almost always mechanical, because those dials exist to show the movement.

Thickness and weight are other clues. Mechanical movements, and especially automatics with their rotor, take up more room than a quartz module, so an automatic is usually a few millimetres thicker than a quartz watch of the same diameter and feels a little heavier on the wrist. A very slim watch is almost always quartz.

## Inside a quartz watch

The heart of a quartz movement is a tiny piece of quartz crystal cut into the shape of a tuning fork. Quartz has a useful property: when an electric current passes through it, it vibrates, and it does so at a very stable frequency. In watches, the crystal is made to vibrate 32,768 times per second, a number chosen because it can be halved fifteen times to give exactly one pulse per second.

An integrated circuit counts those vibrations and, once a second, sends a pulse to a tiny stepping motor. The motor turns a small train of gears that moves the hands, which is why the seconds hand jumps once a second. The battery, usually a small silver-oxide button cell, powers both the crystal and the motor, and typically lasts 2 to 3 years.

Because the crystal's frequency is so stable, a quartz watch is very accurate: a typical one gains or loses only a few seconds a month. Quartz watches were introduced to the public in 1969, after years of research by watchmakers in Japan and Switzerland, and within a few years they had changed the watch industry, making accurate watches affordable for everyone.

Some quartz watches add features that the circuit makes easy: a chronograph that measures fractions of a second, an alarm, or a date that changes cleanly at midnight. In a quartz chronograph, separate small motors drive the stopwatch hands, which is why a quartz chronograph can be thin, accurate and affordable at the same time. The trade-off is that a quartz module is usually replaced rather than repaired when it eventually fails.

## Inside a mechanical watch

A mechanical movement is a small machine with no electronics at all. Its energy comes from the mainspring, a long, thin coiled spring inside a drum called the barrel. Winding the watch tightens the spring; as it slowly unwinds, it turns a train of gears that drives the hands.

Left to itself, the spring would unwind in seconds. What slows it down and divides its energy into equal parts is the escapement, a pair of parts called the escape wheel and the pallet fork. The escapement releases the gear train one tooth at a time, in step with the swings of the balance wheel, a small weighted wheel with a fine hairspring that oscillates back and forth several times a second.

*A Hugo Boss Principle automatic: the skeleton dial shows the wheels and the beating balance.*

Each swing of the balance lets the escape wheel advance by one step, which you hear as the tick of a mechanical watch. The speed of those swings decides how fast the watch runs, which is why the balance and its hairspring are the parts a watchmaker adjusts when regulating a watch. Typical mechanical movements beat 6 or 8 times per second, which is why their seconds hands appear to glide.

All of this happens in a space smaller than a coin, with parts measured in fractions of a millimetre. That is why mechanical movements are admired as craftsmanship, and why a skeleton dial, which cuts away the dial to reveal the wheels and the beating balance, is a natural design for automatic watches. Looking at one for the first time is the best explanation of how a watch works.

## Jewels and why they matter

Turn a mechanical watch over and you may see small red stones set into the movement. These are jewels: synthetic rubies used as bearings for the turning axles of the wheels and the balance. Ruby is extremely hard and smooth, so the steel pivots that turn in it suffer very little friction and wear, which helps the watch keep good time for longer.

A typical mechanical movement has around 17 to 25 jewels, each placed where friction matters most. They are not gemstones in the jewellery sense and add no value as stones; their value is mechanical. Synthetic rubies have been used this way for more than a century, because they can be made to exact sizes and are harder than the steel that turns in them. More jewels do not automatically mean a better watch, beyond the number the movement actually needs.

## Inside an automatic watch

An automatic watch is a mechanical watch with one extra part: the rotor, a half-moon-shaped weight mounted on the movement that swings freely as you move your wrist. Through a set of small gears, the rotor's motion winds the mainspring, so the watch keeps itself wound as long as you wear it. A slipping clutch prevents the spring from being over-wound.

The energy an automatic can store is called its power reserve. Many store enough for one to two days; some modern movements go much further, such as the Tissot Powermatic 80, named for its reserve of up to 80 hours. When the reserve runs out, the watch stops; a few gentle movements, or a few turns of the crown on models that allow it, restart it.

The rotor is often visible through a glass case back, where you can watch it spin as you turn the watch in your hand. On some models it is decorated with patterns or the brand's name. Its weight and its free swing are also why an automatic watch can feel as if something is moving inside when you shake it gently; that is normal, not a loose part.

## Power reserve and accuracy

The power reserve shapes how you live with a mechanical watch. A watch with a 40-hour reserve, worn during the day, will still be running when you pick it up the next morning, but not after a weekend in a drawer. A watch with an 80-hour reserve can rest over a weekend and still be running on Monday.

Accuracy is where quartz has the clear advantage. A mechanical watch is typically accurate to within several seconds a day, and its rate changes slightly with temperature, position and how fully the spring is wound. The Swiss chronometer standard set by COSC allows a certified mechanical movement an average daily rate between minus 4 and plus 6 seconds; most fashion automatics are not certified and run a little wider. In daily life, that means setting the watch every week or two.

## Complications: dates, chronographs and more

Watchmakers call any function beyond the hours, minutes and seconds a complication. The most common is the date, shown through a small window on the dial; on many watches it does not know the length of each month, so it needs correcting after a 30-day month and after February. Others include the day of the week, a 24-hour hand for a second time zone, and the chronograph, or stopwatch.

In a quartz watch, complications are added through the circuit and small motors, which keeps them affordable and accurate. In a mechanical watch, each complication is a set of extra wheels, levers and springs added to the movement, which is why a mechanical chronograph is a far more complex machine than a mechanical three-hand watch. Whatever the movement, avoid setting the date in the hours around midnight, when the date mechanism is working.

## Caring for each kind

A quartz watch asks for very little: a new battery every 2 to 3 years, changed promptly when the seconds hand starts jumping in 4-second steps, so a weak battery never leaks. Within the warranty, battery changes are free at our two Cairo stores.

A mechanical or automatic watch asks for three things. Wear it regularly, or wind it, so it keeps running. Keep it away from strong magnets, such as speakers and magnetic phone cases, which can magnetise the hairspring and make the watch run fast, and from hard knocks, which can disturb the balance.

Store mechanical watches dial up or crown down overnight if you notice a small gain or loss: their rate changes slightly with position, and you can correct small differences this way without touching the crown. A watch winder, a box that turns an automatic while you are not wearing it, is a convenience for people who own several automatics, but not a necessity. And have it serviced every few years, when a watchmaker cleans the movement, replaces the old oils and adjusts its rate.

## Which one suits you

If you want a watch that is always right and ready, needs almost no attention and can be as slim as you like, choose quartz. It is the movement inside most fashion watches, including almost all Michael Kors, Guess and Tommy Hilfiger models in our shop, and nearly all chronographs.

If you want to own a small machine that runs on its own energy, that you can watch working through a skeleton dial or a glass back, and that rewards being worn every day, choose an automatic. Models such as the Hugo Boss Principle, the Emporio Armani Sea Explorer and the Tissot PRX Powermatic 80 bring that experience to everyday watches. Owning one of each lets you choose by the day.

There is no wrong answer, only a better fit. Think about how many watches you own, how often you swap between them, how much you enjoy small rituals such as setting the time, and whether you like the idea of seeing the mechanism at work. A first watch is usually easiest as quartz; a second or third is often the moment to discover a mechanical one. ## Frequently asked questions

### How does a quartz watch work?

A battery makes a tiny quartz crystal vibrate 32,768 times per second. An integrated circuit counts those vibrations and sends a pulse once a second to a small stepping motor, which moves the hands through a set of gears. That is why a quartz watch's seconds hand jumps once a second, and why it is so accurate.

### How does a mechanical watch work?

A wound mainspring stores energy and turns a train of gears. The escapement releases that energy one step at a time, in rhythm with the swings of a balance wheel and its hairspring, which act as the watch's regulator. Each swing produces the tick, and the speed of the swings decides how fast the watch runs.

### What is the difference between mechanical and automatic?

An automatic is a mechanical watch with a rotor: a weight that swings as you move and winds the mainspring for you. A hand-wound mechanical watch has no rotor and must be wound through the crown, usually once a day. Both use the same springs, gears and balance wheel inside.

### What are the jewels in a watch?

Jewels are synthetic rubies used as bearings for the turning axles in a mechanical movement. Ruby is very hard and smooth, so it reduces friction and wear, helping the watch stay accurate for longer. They have no value as gemstones; a typical mechanical movement uses around 17 to 25 of them.
