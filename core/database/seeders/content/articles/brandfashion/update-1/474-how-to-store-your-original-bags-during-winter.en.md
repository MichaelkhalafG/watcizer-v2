Every winter, part of a bag collection goes into the wardrobe: the light-coloured summer totes, the beach bags, the pale crossbodies that do not suit dark coats. A few months later they come out again, and that is when people discover a crease that will not go away, a handle that has left a mark on the side, a sticky patch where two bags touched, or a musty smell from the lining. All of these come from how a bag was stored, and all of them can be avoided. This guide explains how to prepare a bag for storage, how to keep its shape, where and how to store it, how to deal with humidity in Egyptian homes, and how to bring it back into use in spring.

## Key points

- Clean and empty every bag before storing it; dirt and crumbs left inside for 3 or 4 months set in.
- Stuff bags with acid-free tissue paper or a soft cloth to keep their shape; never with newspaper.
- Store bags upright in a breathable cotton dust bag, never in a plastic bag, and not touching each other.
- Do not hang bags by their handles or straps for months; the weight stretches them.
- Keep a sachet of silica gel inside, and open and air your stored bags once a month.

## Prepare the bag before storing it

The most important step happens before the bag goes into the wardrobe. Empty it completely, including the small inner pockets, where receipts, tissues, coins and lipsticks tend to hide. A lipstick left in a pocket for a whole winter can melt or leak, and an old receipt can transfer ink onto the lining. Turn the bag upside down over a bin and shake out crumbs and dust.

Then clean it according to its material. Wipe leather with a dry, soft cloth, and for smooth leather apply a light coat of conditioner a day before storing, so the leather goes into storage supple rather than dry. Wipe coated canvas with a barely damp cloth and dry it well. Treat any stains now, because a mark that sits for 3 or 4 months is much harder to remove than a fresh one.

Finally, let the bag air for a day, open, in a dry room away from the sun. Storing a bag while it is still slightly damp from cleaning or from the last outing is the most common cause of mould and smells in stored bags.

It also helps to make a short list before the bags go away, with 1 line per bag: the bag, its colour, where it is stored and anything you noticed, such as a small mark or a loose thread. When spring comes, the list tells you where everything is, and it reminds you which bag needs a repair before it is used again.

## Keep its shape

A bag that is stored empty and flat will collapse, and the folds it forms over several months can become permanent creases, especially in soft leather. Stuff the body of the bag with acid-free tissue paper, bubble wrap or a soft, clean cloth until it holds its natural shape without being stretched. Do not use newspaper, which can transfer ink onto light leather and linings.

Fill the corners and the base gently, and if the bag has a flap, put a layer of tissue under it so it does not leave an imprint on the body. For structured bags such as satchels and boxy handbags, shape matters even more, because a dent in a stiff panel can be difficult to remove later. Some bags come with a cardboard insert or a pillow; keep it and use it.

## Handles, straps and chains

Handles and straps need their own care in storage. If a bag has a detachable shoulder strap, remove it and store it flat or loosely rolled inside the bag, rather than leaving it hanging or folded sharply. Lay top handles in their natural position, with a little tissue paper underneath, so they do not press into the body of the bag and leave a mark.

Chain straps are the most likely to cause damage, because metal pressed against leather for months can leave dents and marks, and can scratch neighbouring bags. Wrap chains in a soft cloth or a piece of tissue, and tuck them inside the bag. Metal hardware such as clasps and zip pulls should also be wrapped or turned away from the leather, so that they cannot press into it.

## Choose the right covering

Each bag should go into a breathable cover, ideally a cotton or flannel dust bag, the one it came with if it had one, or a clean cotton pillowcase. Breathable fabric keeps dust off while letting air move, which prevents moisture from building up. Avoid plastic bags and airtight plastic boxes: they trap humidity, which can cause mould on leather and linings, and some plastics can react with coated finishes over time.

Bags should not touch each other in storage. Leather against leather can transfer colour, especially from dark bags onto light ones, and coated canvas or patent finishes pressed together for months can stick. A dust bag for each bag solves both problems.

Label each dust bag so you do not have to open every one to find a bag. A paper tag tied to the drawstring, or a photo of the bag taped to a box, saves time and saves the bags from being handled more than needed.

## Where and how to store

Store bags upright on a shelf, in a wardrobe or in an open box, standing on their base as they would on a table. Hanging bags by their handles or straps is fine for a day but not for 3 or 4 months, because the weight of the bag slowly stretches the handles and can distort the top of the bag. Lying bags flat or stacking them crushes their shape and leaves creases.

Choose a place that is cool, dry, dark and well ventilated. A bedroom wardrobe is usually better than a storage room, a basement or the space under a bed, which are often more humid and dusty. Keep stored bags away from radiators, heaters and windows, and never on top of a wardrobe, where it is warmest and dustiest. Light, even indoor light, fades leather over months, so a closed wardrobe or a box is better than an open shelf.

Measure the shelf before you plan. A shelf at least 30 to 35 cm deep holds most shoulder bags and totes upright without their handles pressing against the wardrobe door, while small crossbodies and wallets can share a drawer, each in its own cover. If space is short, store the largest bags on the lowest shelf, where they can stand without anything on top of them.

## Humidity in Egyptian homes

Humidity is the biggest storage risk in winter, especially in Alexandria, on the North Coast and in homes near the Nile or the sea, where relative humidity often rises above 60 percent. Leather and fabric linings absorb moisture, and if it stays, mould spots and a musty smell follow. Cairo is drier, but closed wardrobes in rooms that are rarely aired can still become damp in the cooler months.

Put a sachet of silica gel inside each stored bag, or at least on each shelf, and replace or dry it every few weeks. Cedar blocks also absorb some moisture and keep the wardrobe smelling fresh. Do not put stored bags in a bathroom or a kitchen cupboard, and if your home is humid, open the wardrobe for an hour on dry days to let the air circulate.

If you do find a white or green spot of mould on a stored bag, take the bag outside, brush the spot gently with a soft, dry brush, and let the bag air in the shade. Wipe the area with a barely damp cloth, let it dry fully, and store it again only when it is completely dry. If the mould returns or has stained the leather, take the bag to a leather specialist.

## Check them every month

Stored bags should not be forgotten. Once a month, open the wardrobe, take each bag out of its dust bag for a few minutes, look at it in good light and let it air. Check for creases, marks, mould spots and smells, and readjust the stuffing if the bag has started to lose its shape. This takes 10 minutes and finds most problems while they are still small.

It is also a good moment to rotate. Moving bags from the back of the shelf to the front, or from the bottom to the top, keeps any one bag from being pressed or forgotten for too long. And if there is a bag you have not used in two seasons, it may be time to give it to someone who will.

Leather also benefits from being used now and then. If winter lasts for several months, take a stored leather bag out for an evening or a day once in the season; being handled and carried keeps the leather supple and the handles in shape, and it is a chance to notice anything that needs attention.

## Light-coloured and summer bags

Light-coloured bags in white, cream, beige and pastel tones need the most care in storage, because they show every mark. Make sure they are spotless before storing, wrap them in white tissue paper, never coloured paper, and keep them well away from dark bags and denim, which can transfer dye. Store them in a white or undyed cotton dust bag.

Straw, raffia and woven summer bags are brittle and can crack if they dry out or are crushed. Stuff them gently, store them upright with nothing on top, and keep them away from heaters. Nylon and canvas beach bags should be washed according to their label, completely dried and then folded loosely, never put away with sand or salt still in the seams.

Patent leather and glossy coated finishes need one extra precaution: they can stick to themselves, to other bags and even to some dust bags in warm, humid conditions. Wrap them in a layer of white tissue paper before they go into their cover, so no glossy surface touches another.

## Bringing bags back in spring

When the weather warms up and you take a bag out of storage, give it a day to air before using it. Remove the stuffing, wipe the outside with a dry, soft cloth, and check the lining and the pockets. Smooth leather that looks a little dull usually comes back to life with a light coat of conditioner, tested first on a hidden area.

If a crease has formed, stuff the bag firmly for a few more days and keep it at room temperature; most light creases in leather relax with time. Avoid using heat, such as a hair dryer or an iron, to remove creases, because heat can damage the leather and the finish. If a mark or a smell remains, a leather specialist can usually treat it.

In Egypt, most people swap their bags back around March or April, when the days warm up and the light summer colours return. Plan a short session for the swap: take the summer bags out, air and check them, and store the winter bags you will not need, such as dark suede or heavy leather, following the same steps in reverse. Half an hour, twice a year, keeps the whole collection in good condition.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I store my bag in a plastic bag?

No. Plastic bags and airtight plastic boxes trap humidity, which can cause mould on leather and linings, and some plastics can react with coated finishes over time. Use a cotton dust bag, the bag's own if it came with one, or a clean cotton pillowcase, which keeps dust off and lets the bag breathe.

### What should I stuff my bag with?

Acid-free tissue paper, bubble wrap or a soft, clean cloth. Fill the body, the corners and the base gently until the bag holds its natural shape without being stretched. Do not use newspaper, which can transfer ink onto leather and linings, and keep any cardboard insert or pillow that came with the bag.

### Can I hang my bags in the wardrobe?

For a day or two, yes, but not for months. Over 3 or 4 months the weight of the bag slowly stretches the handles and straps and can distort the top of the bag. For storage through the winter, stand bags upright on a shelf or in a box, each in its own dust bag.

### How do I stop mould on stored bags?

Store bags clean and completely dry, in breathable cotton dust bags, in a dry wardrobe rather than a storage room or under a bed. Put a sachet of silica gel inside each bag or on each shelf, and air the wardrobe and the bags once a month, especially in humid coastal cities.

### How often should I check stored bags?

Once a month. Take each bag out of its dust bag for a few minutes, look at it in good light, let it air and check for creases, marks, mould and smells. Readjust the stuffing if needed, and rotate the bags on the shelf so that none is pressed or forgotten for too long.
