A chronograph is a watch with a built-in stopwatch. Alongside the normal hours, minutes and seconds, it can measure a stretch of time on demand: you press a pusher to start it, press again to stop it, and press a second pusher to send the hands back to zero. The small dials on the face, called sub-dials or counters, keep track of how many minutes and hours have passed. That is the whole idea, and once you know it, every chronograph becomes easy to read. This guide explains the parts, the scales around the edge, how to use the function day to day, and how to choose one, with chronographs from our own shop, where we currently stock {{count family=watch}} watches.

## Key points

- A chronograph is a watch plus a stopwatch: the top pusher starts and stops it, the bottom pusher resets it.
- The large centre seconds hand is usually the stopwatch hand; the time of day runs on a small sub-dial.
- Some watches have sub-dials for the day and date instead of a stopwatch; those are multifunction watches, not chronographs.
- A tachymeter scale turns the stopwatch into a speed meter over 1 km.
- Running the stopwatch all day uses battery on a quartz model, so stop it when you do not need it.

## The parts of a chronograph

Look at a chronograph from the front and you will see the crown at the 3 o'clock side, usually with a pusher above and below it. The top pusher, at about 2 o'clock, starts and stops the stopwatch. The bottom pusher, at about 4 o'clock, resets it to zero. Between them, the crown sets the time and, on most models, the date.

On the dial, the large hands show the hours and minutes as on any watch. The big seconds hand in the centre is usually reserved for the stopwatch, so it stands still at 12 until you press start. The running seconds of the time of day move instead on one of the small sub-dials, typically at 6 or 9 o'clock. The other sub-dials count the elapsed minutes, often up to 30 or 60, and on many models the elapsed hours, up to 12.

Around the edge, many chronographs carry a scale printed on the bezel or the outer ring of the dial. The most common is the tachymeter, explained further down. A few models add a pulsometer or a telemeter, but at Watchizer the tachymeter is by far the most frequent, especially on sporty steel models such as the [Tommy Hilfiger](/brand/tommy-hilfiger) chronographs.

## How to start, stop and reset

Using the stopwatch takes 3 presses. Press the top pusher once and the centre seconds hand starts sweeping; the minute counter begins to move after the first full turn. Press the top pusher again and everything stops, so you can read the time you measured. Press the bottom pusher and all the stopwatch hands fly back to zero, ready for the next use.

You can also pause and continue. Stop the stopwatch with the top pusher, then press the top pusher again instead of resetting, and it carries on from where it stopped. This is useful for timing something in parts, such as the actual cooking time of a dish that you take off the heat twice. Only the bottom pusher clears the measurement.

Two habits keep a chronograph happy. First, never press the bottom pusher while the stopwatch is running: on most quartz models it does nothing, but on some it can confuse the hands and you will need to realign them. Second, do not press any pusher under water unless the watch is specifically rated for it, because pushers are where water finds its way in. Check the water rating on the product page before you swim with a chronograph.

## Reading the sub-dials correctly

The trickiest part for new owners is knowing which sub-dial shows what. A good rule, which takes about 1 minute, is to start the stopwatch and watch which hands move. The centre seconds hand sweeps round; after 1 minute, one sub-dial ticks forward by one mark, and that is your minute counter. The sub-dial whose hand moves all the time, even when the stopwatch is stopped, is the running seconds of the time of day.

Reading a measurement then works like reading a clock in three parts. Take the elapsed hours from the hour counter, if there is one, the elapsed minutes from the minute counter, and the seconds from the centre hand. On a typical 30-minute counter, each mark is 1 minute; on a 60-minute counter the marks are closer together, so read them slowly the first few times.

Some fashion watches look like chronographs but are not. Their sub-dials show the day of the week, the date and a 24-hour indicator instead of elapsed time, and their pushers, if any, adjust those displays. They are called multifunction watches. Both styles are attractive; just know which one you are buying. Every product title and description at Watchizer states whether a model is a chronograph or a multifunction watch.

## The tachymeter, explained simply

A tachymeter is a scale that converts the time taken to cover 1 km into a speed in kilometres per hour. Start the stopwatch as you pass a kilometre marker on the road, stop it at the next one, and read the number on the scale next to the centre seconds hand. If the hand stops at 30 seconds, the scale reads 120, meaning you averaged 120 km/h. At 45 seconds it reads 80, and at 60 seconds, 60.

Because the scale works for anything that happens at a regular rate, you can also use it for production. If you time how long it takes to pack 1 box, the scale shows how many boxes per hour that rate gives. It is not something most owners use every day, but it explains the numbers around the dial, and it is a nice trick to show a friend on the Cairo to Alexandria desert road, with someone else driving.

The tachymeter only works within one turn of the seconds hand, so it reads speeds above 60 km/h. For slower speeds the scale simply runs out. Many sports and driving-inspired watches at Watchizer carry one, including the [Hugo Boss](/brand/hugo-boss) and Maserati chronographs.

## Quartz or automatic chronographs

Almost every chronograph at Watchizer runs on quartz. A quartz chronograph is accurate, light and slim for a stopwatch watch, and it needs only a battery every 2 years or so. Its stopwatch typically measures to a fraction of a second on a small additional counter, which is more precision than most people will ever use.

The idea is older than the wristwatch. Louis Moinet built a timer for astronomical observation in 1816, and Nicolas Rieussec made one for timing horse races in 1821, which gave the chronograph its name, from the Greek for time and writing. Mechanical, automatic chronographs still exist today. They are more complex inside, thicker, and more expensive to service, because the stopwatch adds dozens of parts to the movement. Their appeal is the craft and the smooth sweep of the hands. If you want a mechanical movement first and a stopwatch second, our [automatic watches page](/subtypes/automatic) gathers every self-winding model we sell.

For daily wear, a quartz chronograph is the practical answer. It survives being left in a drawer for weeks, keeps time within seconds a month, and needs no winding. Just remember that running the stopwatch continuously, as some owners do because they like the sweeping hand, drains the battery faster. Stop it when you are not using it.

## Everyday uses you will actually find

The stopwatch is more useful than it sounds. In the kitchen, it times pasta, eggs or rice without a phone in floury hands. In the car park, it tells you how long you have been parked. At the gym, it times rest between sets, and on a run it gives you your lap times around the club. Parents use it for children's swimming lessons, and students for timed practice questions before an exam.

It also helps at work, often in ways people only notice after a few weeks of wearing one. A pharmacist times a preparation, a teacher times a speaking exercise, a mechanic times a test drive. Because the chronograph is on your wrist, you start it without unlocking anything, which is why many owners end up using theirs more than they expected. For Ramadan, it is a handy way to count down the last minutes before iftar.

The countdown use needs a little arithmetic, since a chronograph counts up rather than down. Start it at the moment you want to measure from, and read how long has passed. If you want a true countdown with an alarm, a digital model, such as those on our [digital watches page](/subtypes/digital-watch), is the better tool.

## How to choose a chronograph

Start with size. A chronograph needs room for its sub-dials, so men's models are usually 42 to 46 mm and women's 36 to 40 mm. If your wrist is slim, look at the smaller end and check the thickness, since stopwatch movements make cases a little taller. A steel bracelet balances the extra weight; leather and rubber feel lighter.

Then choose the look. A white or silver dial with dark sub-dials is easy to read and looks sporty. A black dial with matching sub-dials looks calmer and more formal. Blue is the most popular choice at Watchizer, a middle ground that suits both a suit and jeans. Gold and rose gold chronographs, many of them women's models from Michael Kors, turn the stopwatch into jewellery.

## Caring for the pushers and the battery

Pushers are moving parts with seals, so treat them kindly. Press them firmly but do not force them, and avoid using them with wet or sandy fingers after the beach. Rinse a sport chronograph with fresh water after the sea, with the pushers untouched, and dry it with a soft cloth.

When the battery runs low, many quartz chronographs warn you: the small seconds hand starts jumping in steps of several seconds. Bring the watch to one of our stores; we change the battery, check the seals and, if the stopwatch hands no longer return exactly to zero after the change, we realign them. That realignment is normal after a battery change and takes a few minutes.

If the stopwatch hand stops a fraction short of zero after a knock, the same realignment fixes it. Most models have a procedure using the crown and the pushers, described in the instruction leaflet; our team can do it for you if you prefer. ## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between a chronograph and a stopwatch?

A stopwatch is a device that only measures elapsed time. A chronograph is a watch that tells the time and also has a built-in stopwatch, started and stopped with the pushers on the side of the case. So every chronograph contains a stopwatch, but a stopwatch on its own is not a watch.

### Why does the big seconds hand not move on my chronograph?

On most chronographs the large centre seconds hand belongs to the stopwatch, so it waits at 12 until you press the start pusher. The running seconds of the time of day are shown on a small sub-dial instead. Watch the sub-dials for a minute and you will see which one is always moving.

### Can I leave the chronograph running all the time?

You can, but on a quartz chronograph it uses the battery faster, so it will need changing sooner than the usual 2 years or so. It can also make the watch harder to read at a glance. Most owners start the stopwatch when they need it, then stop and reset it.

### Is a chronograph harder to look after than a normal watch?

Not really. A quartz chronograph needs the same care as any quartz watch: a battery every couple of years and a seal check at the same time. The only extra rules are to avoid pressing the pushers under water and to have the hands realigned if they drift from zero after a battery change.

### How do I know if a watch is a chronograph or multifunction?

Look at the product title and description: at Watchizer every model is described as a chronograph or a multifunction watch. On the watch itself, press the top pusher: if the centre seconds hand starts sweeping and a counter moves after a minute, it is a chronograph. Multifunction sub-dials show the day and date instead.
