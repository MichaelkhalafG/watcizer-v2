Choose a quartz watch if you want one that is always accurate and ready, needs only a battery every few years and can sit in a drawer for weeks without stopping; choose an automatic if you want a mechanical watch that winds itself on your wrist, shows its movement and rewards you for wearing it every day. The best way to decide is to imagine living with each one: over a day, a week, a holiday and five years. That is what this guide does, using the quartz and automatic watches in our shop as examples. We carry {{count family=watch}} watches at Watchizer, and most of them are quartz.

## Key points

- Quartz watches run on a battery, keep time within seconds a month and need almost no attention.
- Automatic watches are mechanical, wind themselves as you move and stop after a day or two off the wrist.
- Over five years, a quartz watch needs one or two battery changes; an automatic benefits from one service.
- If you rotate several watches, quartz is more practical; if you wear one watch every day, an automatic stays wound.
- At Watchizer automatics appear among Fossil, Hugo Boss, Emporio Armani and Tissot models; most other watches are quartz.

## Two ways of keeping time

Inside every analogue watch is a movement, the engine that measures time and turns the hands. A quartz movement is electronic: a small battery makes a quartz crystal vibrate at a precise frequency, and a circuit converts those vibrations into one step of the hands every second. An automatic movement is mechanical: a spring stores energy, a balance wheel releases it in regular beats, and a weighted rotor winds the spring as your wrist moves.

The two feel different from the first moment. A quartz watch's seconds hand ticks once a second; an automatic's moves in many small steps and seems to sweep. An automatic is often a little thicker and heavier, because of its rotor, and many show their movement through an open dial or a glass case back. A quartz watch can be slimmer and lighter, and most fashion chronographs use quartz movements.

There is also a third kind, the hand-wound mechanical watch, which has no rotor and must be wound by turning the crown, usually once a day. It is the traditional form of the mechanical watch and is now rare among fashion brands, so in this guide we compare the two kinds you will actually meet in our shop: quartz and automatic.

## A day with each

Morning, 7 o'clock. You pick up your quartz watch from the bedside table, and it shows the exact time: it ran all night on its battery. You pick up your automatic, and it shows the right time too, because you wore it yesterday and its power reserve carried it through the night. Both are ready.

During the day, you will hardly notice the difference in use. Both tell the time, both can be water resistant to the same rating and both look good with a shirt. What you may notice is the automatic's sweeping seconds hand and, on a skeleton model, the movement beating behind the dial. It is easy to find yourself looking at it simply for the pleasure of it.

In the evening you take both off. The quartz watch keeps running whatever you do; the automatic keeps running for as long as its power reserve lasts, which for many models is a day or two.

That small difference shapes the whole relationship with the watch. A quartz watch is a tool that is always there; an automatic is a companion that asks to be worn. Neither is better, but one of them will suit your habits more naturally, and that is the one you will enjoy for years.

## A week with each

Over a week, the difference becomes clearer. If you wear the automatic every day, it stays wound and keeps running without any help from you. Its accuracy will drift by a few seconds a day, so by the end of the week it may be a minute or so fast or slow, and you set it again in a few seconds by pulling out the crown.

The quartz watch, over the same week, drifts by a second or two at most. You never set it, unless the clocks change. If you switch between several watches during the week, the quartz ones are always ready; an automatic left on the dresser for three days will have stopped and needs setting before you wear it.

Restarting an automatic is simple. Pick it up, give it a few gentle swings or, on models that allow it, wind it through the crown with a few turns, then set the time and the date if it has one. Set the date away from the hours around midnight, when the date mechanism is working, to avoid straining it, a sensible habit with any watch that has a date.

## A holiday with each

On holiday, the quartz watch is the easy companion. It does not mind being left in a hotel room while you swim, it keeps the time while you sleep late, and it copes with knocks during sport. If you change time zones, you set it once and forget it.

The same is true of the strap. A quartz watch on a rubber or silicone strap is the ideal beach and pool companion, because it rinses clean and does not mind sand or sweat. Leather straps on either kind of watch should stay dry, so keep them for evenings out rather than days by the water.

An automatic needs a little more thought. Wear it every day and it will run happily; leave it in the safe for the beach days and you will set it each evening. Keep it away from the knocks of tennis or a fall, which a mechanical movement tolerates less well than quartz, and away from strong magnets, such as speakers and magnetic phone cases. Check the water rating of either watch before swimming: 50 m allows a short pool swim, 100 m or more the sea.

## Five years with each

Over five years, the costs of ownership differ in kind. A quartz watch needs a new battery every two to three years, usually one or two changes over the period. Within the warranty we replace batteries free of charge at our two Cairo stores; after that, a battery change is a quick, inexpensive job. Beyond the battery, a quartz watch asks only for care of its case and strap.

An automatic needs no battery at all, but its many moving parts are lubricated with fine oils that age. A service every few years, in which a watchmaker cleans, oils and adjusts the movement, keeps it accurate and protects it for decades. Think of it like a car: a quartz watch is a reliable electric runabout, an automatic a classic engine that rewards regular care.

There is also the question of how long each watch will last. A well-made quartz movement can run for many years, but when it eventually fails it is usually replaced rather than repaired. A mechanical movement can, in principle, be serviced and repaired again and again, which is why mechanical watches are often handed down. If you like the idea of a watch that could one day belong to your son or daughter, an automatic fits that idea well.

## Accuracy in real life

Quartz is far more accurate: a typical quartz watch is within a few seconds a month. A mechanical watch is typically within several seconds a day, and its rate shifts a little with temperature, position and how fully wound it is. For reference, the Swiss COSC chronometer standard allows an average daily rate of minus 4 to plus 6 seconds; most fashion automatics are not certified and run a little wider.

In practice this rarely matters for daily life. You set an automatic every week or two, which takes seconds, and it is never far enough out to make you late. If you need precise time for work, such as timing trains or meetings to the minute, quartz is the better tool; if the pleasure of the mechanism matters more, an automatic is worth the small adjustment.

Accuracy also depends on habit. An automatic worn every day runs more consistently than one worn now and then, because its spring stays well wound. Placing it overnight with the dial up or the crown down can shift its rate slightly in one direction or the other, and you can use this to correct small gains or losses without touching the crown.

## What the price difference pays for

Automatic watches usually cost more than quartz watches from the same brand. The difference pays for the mechanical movement itself: dozens of small parts, assembled and adjusted, with a rotor and a balance wheel that keep working without a battery. On skeleton and open-heart models, such as the Fossil automatics or the Hugo Boss Principle, it also pays for the work of cutting away the dial to show that movement.

A quartz watch spends its budget elsewhere: on the case, the bracelet, the dial and features such as a precise chronograph. That is why a quartz chronograph and an automatic three-hand watch from the same brand can sit close in price. Compare them by what you value, the function and finish of the quartz or the mechanism of the automatic, rather than by price alone.

## How to choose

Choose quartz, such as a Tommy Hilfiger or a Michael Kors chronograph, if you own several watches and switch between them, if you want a slim watch for shirts and suits, if you play sport with your watch on, or if you simply want a watch you never have to think about. Choose automatic if you wear one watch most days, if you enjoy looking at the movement, if you like the idea of a mechanical object that can last for generations, and if setting the time now and then is a pleasure rather than a chore.

A practical answer is to own both: a quartz watch for sport, travel and days when they want to forget about it, and an automatic for the days when they want to enjoy it. If you are buying your first watch, a quartz model is the easiest start; if you are buying your second or third, an automatic adds something genuinely new to your collection.

Your wrist and wardrobe matter too. A slim wrist and fitted shirts favour a thinner watch, which is usually quartz. A larger wrist and casual clothes carry the extra thickness of an automatic easily. If you work with your hands, play sport often or travel constantly, quartz is the practical companion; if your days are calmer and you enjoy small rituals, an automatic fits naturally into them.

Finally, think about the dial. Skeleton and open-heart dials, which show the movement, exist only on mechanical watches, so if that look attracts you, the choice is made. If you prefer a clean, readable dial or a busy chronograph, quartz offers the widest choice in our shop, in every metal, size and colour.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is an automatic watch better than a quartz watch?

Neither is better overall. Quartz is more accurate, cheaper to own and ready whenever you pick it up. Automatic is mechanical, needs no battery and offers the pleasure of a visible movement and a sweeping seconds hand. Choose quartz for convenience and automatic for craft, depending on how you wear your watches.

### Does an automatic watch stop if I do not wear it?

Yes, after its power reserve runs out, which for many models is a day or two; some, such as the Tissot Powermatic 80, run for up to 80 hours. To restart it, give it a few gentle movements or wind it through the crown if the model allows, and set the time.

### How often does a quartz watch need a new battery?

Usually every two to three years, a little sooner for a chronograph whose stopwatch is used often. When the seconds hand starts jumping in four-second steps, the battery is low. Within the warranty we replace it free of charge at our two Cairo stores, usually while you wait.

### Do automatic watches need servicing?

Yes, every few years. A watchmaker cleans, oils and adjusts the movement, which keeps it accurate and protects its parts. Keep an automatic away from strong magnets and hard knocks between services, and wear it regularly so it stays wound.
