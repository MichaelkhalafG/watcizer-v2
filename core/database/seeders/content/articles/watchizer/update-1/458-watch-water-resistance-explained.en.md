A watch's water rating tells you where it can safely go, not how deep you can dive with it: 30 m (3 ATM) means splashes and rain only, 50 m (5 ATM) allows a short pool swim, 100 m (10 ATM) is fine for swimming in the sea and snorkelling, and 200 m (20 ATM) suits regular water sports. The numbers come from a laboratory pressure test, which is why they sound deeper than what the watch can do in daily life. We carry {{count family=watch}} watches at Watchizer, most rated to 30 or 50 m, and this guide explains what each rating means, why heat matters as much as depth and how to keep a watch water resistant for years.

## Key points

- 30 m (3 ATM): splashes, rain and hand washing only; no swimming.
- 50 m (5 ATM): a short swim in a pool; no diving, no water sports.
- 100 m (10 ATM): swimming in the sea and snorkelling; 200 m (20 ATM) for regular water sports.
- Never pull out the crown or press chronograph pushers under water, and keep watches out of hot showers and saunas.
- Seals age: have the water resistance checked every year or two if you swim with your watch, and after any battery change.

## Why the numbers sound deeper than they are

A water rating such as 50 m is the result of a static pressure test: the watch is held still under a pressure equal to that depth of water for a short time, and it must not let water in. Real life is not static. When you dive into a pool, swim strokes, jump from a boat or turn on a powerful tap, the water hits the watch with sudden pressure peaks that can exceed the test pressure for an instant.

That is why the industry's own guidance treats the number as a category rather than a depth. A watch rated to 30 m will survive a fall into shallow water but is not designed for swimming; a watch rated to 50 m is designed for a swim, not for diving. An international ISO standard for water-resistant watches describes these tests, and divers' watches follow a stricter ISO standard of their own.

The units vary but mean the same thing. 30 m, 3 ATM and 3 bar are the same rating; so are 50 m, 5 ATM and 5 bar, and 100 m, 10 ATM and 10 bar. Many watches show it on the case back, and every product page in our shop states it.

A watch with no rating at all, or one marked only "water resistant" without a number, should be treated as splash-proof at best. Some very decorative watches, such as jewellery watches with stones set in the case, are rated lower than plain steel watches, because their construction leaves more joints for water to find. When in doubt, read the product page or ask us before the watch meets water.

## 30 m (3 ATM): splashes and rain

A rating of 30 m is the minimum most fashion watches carry, and it covers daily life: washing your hands, rain, splashes from the sink and sweat. It does not cover swimming, showering or bathing. Many dress watches and women's watches with small cases are rated to 30 m, because slim cases and decorative crowns leave less room for heavy seals.

With a 30 m watch, take it off before swimming, the shower or the bath, and push the crown in fully before you wash your hands. If you get caught in a downpour, simply dry the watch with a soft cloth afterwards. A 30 m rating is perfectly adequate for a watch you wear to the office and to dinners.

Be careful with everyday situations that look harmless. Washing the car with a hose, watering the garden or cleaning the kitchen with a strong jet of water can put more pressure on a watch than a calm swim. With a 30 m watch, take it off for these jobs, or at least keep the crown pushed in and the watch away from the jet.

## 50 m (5 ATM): a short swim

A rating of 50 m is the most common among the watches we sell, and it adds a short swim in a pool to everything a 30 m watch can do. It is the practical rating for most people: you do not need to think about the watch in the rain, when washing up or during a swim on holiday.

What 50 m does not cover is diving from a board, water sports such as jet-skiing, or long sessions in the sea. If you swim often, or spend whole days at the beach, choose 100 m. And whatever the rating, rinse the watch in fresh water after a chlorinated pool or the sea, because salt and chlorine attack seals and straps over time.

A 50 m watch is also the right choice for the gym and outdoor sport, where sweat is the main exposure. Sweat contains salt, just like the sea, so wipe the watch after training and rinse a rubber or silicone strap now and then. A steel bracelet benefits from the same rinse, which stops salt crystals forming between the links.

## 100 m and 200 m (10 and 20 ATM): sea and sport

A rating of 100 m is the first that suits real swimming in the sea and snorkelling. These watches usually have stronger cases and seals, sometimes a screw-down crown, and they often come on steel bracelets or rubber straps that cope with salt water. Sporty models from brands such as Emporio Armani's Sea Explorer line are built in this spirit.

A rating of 200 m covers regular water sports, including snorkelling for hours and jumping into the water. True diving watches for scuba diving follow the stricter ISO standard for divers' watches and are marked "Diver's" with a depth; they are tested more strictly than ordinary water-resistant watches. For most people, 100 m is enough for any holiday by the sea.

## A day at the Egyptian coast

A summer day on the North Coast or the Red Sea is the toughest test most watches face: sun, salt water, sand, sunscreen and sudden plunges from hot to cool. A watch rated to 100 m on a rubber strap or a steel bracelet is the ideal companion. A 50 m watch can join you for a short swim in the hotel pool, but leave it in your bag when you snorkel or spend hours in the sea.

Sand is the hidden enemy at the beach. Fine grains work their way under the bezel, into the clasp and around the crown, and they scratch polished surfaces. Rinse the watch in fresh water as soon as you can, ideally in a bowl or under a gentle tap, and never wipe a sandy glass with a dry cloth. Keep sunscreen off the case and strap, because it attacks gaskets and leaves a film that collects sand.

Finally, keep the watch out of the sun when you are not wearing it. A watch left on a sun lounger or in a closed car can become very hot, which ages seals and fades dials and straps. A bag, a pocket or the shade under a towel is enough.

## The crown, the pushers and the case back

Water enters a watch through its openings: the crown, the chronograph pushers and the joint of the case back. The crown is the most important. Always push it in fully before your watch meets water, and never pull it out to set the time while the watch is wet. If your watch has a screw-down crown, screw it down firmly after every adjustment.

On a chronograph, never press the pushers under water, even on a 100 m watch, unless the model is specifically designed for it. Pressing them opens a tiny path for water past their seals. Start and stop the stopwatch before you enter the water, not while swimming.

The crown deserves a quick check every morning if you swim often. Run your fingertip over it to make sure it is fully pushed in, and on a screw-down crown, make sure it is screwed down. A crown left pulled out by accident, after setting the date for example, is an easy way for water to enter a watch that should have been perfectly safe.

The case back is sealed by a gasket, a rubber ring that is compressed when the back is closed. It is opened at every battery change, which is why a battery change should always be done by someone who checks and, if needed, replaces the gasket afterwards. ## Heat, steam and sudden changes

Heat is as much a threat as depth. Hot showers, saunas at 80 °C or more, steam rooms and hot tubs make the metal and the gaskets expand at different rates, and steam passes where cold water would not. A watch that swims happily in a cool pool can let moisture in during a hot shower. Take every watch off before hot water and steam.

Sudden changes of temperature are the other risk. Jumping into cold water after lying in the sun, or walking from summer heat into strong air conditioning with a wet watch, stresses the seals. If you ever see a mist or droplets inside the glass, bring the watch in at once; moisture inside a case can damage the movement within days, and a quick check is far cheaper than a repair.

Washing up is a hidden source of heat as well. Hot water from the tap, combined with detergent, is hard on seals and on plating, so it is worth taking the watch off before a long session at the sink. The same applies to cooking over steaming pots, which can bring moisture and heat close to the watch for a long time.

## Straps and water

The case is only half of the story; the strap matters too. Steel bracelets and rubber or silicone straps, as on the Tommy Hilfiger Bank 1791722, cope with water, sweat and salt. Leather does not: water darkens it, makes it stiff and shortens its life, whatever the case is rated for. If you want a watch for swimming, choose a steel bracelet or a rubber strap, and keep leather-strap watches for dry days and evenings.

After the sea or a pool, rinse the case and the strap in fresh water, paying attention to the buckle, the clasp and the gap between the lugs, where salt and sand collect. Dry the watch with a soft towel and let a rubber strap air-dry before you put the watch away.

If you love your leather-strap watch and want to wear it on holiday, consider a second strap in rubber or silicone for the beach days. Many watches use standard strap widths, so a watchmaker can usually fit one in minutes; bring the watch in and we will tell you what fits. Work outside the warranty is always quoted before it starts.

## Seals age: when to check

Water resistance is not permanent. Gaskets dry out and lose their elasticity with time, heat and contact with chemicals such as chlorine, sunscreen and perfume. A watch that was water resistant on the day you bought it may not be after a few years of swimming.

If you swim with your watch often, have its water resistance checked every 1 or 2 years, and always after the case has been opened for a battery change. If you rarely swim with it, a check every few years is enough. Ask us at either of our stores whether your model's case can be tested, and what the test involves.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I swim with a 50 m water-resistant watch?

Yes, for a short swim in a pool. A 50 m (5 ATM) rating is designed for swimming, not for diving from a board, water sports or long days in the sea. Push the crown in fully first, never press the pushers under water, and rinse the watch in fresh water afterwards. For regular sea swimming, choose 100 m.

### Can I shower with my watch?

It is better not to, even with a high rating. Hot water and steam make the case and gaskets expand and can let moisture in, and soap and shampoo attack the seals. Take your watch off before the shower, the bath, saunas and steam rooms, and put it back on once your wrist is dry.

### What is the difference between ATM, bar and metres?

They are the same rating in different units. 3 ATM, 3 bar and 30 m are the same; so are 5 ATM, 5 bar and 50 m, and 10 ATM, 10 bar and 100 m. The number comes from a static pressure test and should be read as a category of use, not as a diving depth.

### Does water resistance last forever?

No. The rubber gaskets that seal the case age with time, heat and chemicals such as chlorine and sunscreen. If you swim with your watch often, have its water resistance checked every year or two, and always after a battery change has opened the case back.

### What should I do if water gets inside my watch?

Bring it in at once. Moisture or mist inside the glass means water has entered the case, and it can damage the movement within days. Do not open the watch yourself or leave it in the sun to dry. Message us on WhatsApp or visit one of our stores, and we will check it and tell you what is needed.
