An automatic watch is a small machine on your wrist. A weighted rotor turns as you move, winds a spring, and the spring releases its energy through a chain of gears at a steady beat. This page explains what to expect from one, how to live with it, and how the models we carry differ.

## How an automatic works

Inside the case, a half-moon rotor spins freely around the movement. Every turn tightens the mainspring a little, and a balance wheel swinging several times a second lets that energy out in precise steps. A full spring keeps most automatics running for around 40 hours off the wrist. The result is a seconds hand that glides instead of jumping once a second, which is the first thing most owners notice.

## Accuracy you can expect

A quartz watch drifts a few seconds a month; a good automatic drifts a few seconds a day. That is normal for a mechanical movement and depends on position, temperature and how fully the watch is wound. Setting the time once or twice a week is part of owning one. Tissot movements and the better Hugo Boss calibres are among the most consistent in our range.

Hugo Boss brings two very different automatics. ## Size and fit

Automatic cases on this page measure from 38 to 44 mm. Because a mechanical movement is deeper than a quartz one, an automatic sits a little higher on the wrist, often 12 to 14 mm. A steel bracelet balances that weight well, while leather keeps it lighter. If your wrist is under 17 cm, aim for 38 to 41 mm.

## Winding and setting

If the watch has stopped, turn the crown clockwise about 20 to 30 times to give the spring a start, then set the time and wear it. Avoid setting the date between about 9 pm and 3 am, when the date-change gears are engaged, because forcing them can damage the movement. Once on the wrist, normal daily movement keeps the watch running.

## Living with an automatic

Wear it daily and it simply runs. If you own several watches, a watch winder keeps an automatic going between wears. Keep it away from strong magnets, which can make it run fast, and avoid hard knocks such as golf or tennis swings. Water resistance follows the rating on the product page, usually 50 to 100 m for the models here.

## Service over the years

An automatic needs a service every 4 to 5 years: the movement is cleaned, oiled and regulated, and the seals are replaced. In return it never needs a battery and can be passed from one generation to the next. Our [warranty page](/warranty) explains the cover of up to 3 years and what it includes.

## Questions and answers

### Why has my automatic watch stopped?

It has run out of stored energy, usually after a day or two off the wrist. Turn the crown about 20 to 30 times, set the time and put it on; normal movement then keeps it running. If it stops again while you wear it daily, bring it to one of our stores so the team can check the winding.

### Is an automatic watch accurate?

It is accurate within a few seconds a day, which is normal for a mechanical movement. A quartz watch is more precise, but many people prefer the craft and the sweeping seconds of an automatic. Checking the time against your phone once a week and adjusting it is part of the pleasure of owning one.

### Can I wear an automatic every day?

Yes, and that is the best way to keep it running. Wear it to work and on weekends, take it off for heavy sport, and keep it away from strong magnets. A steel bracelet copes with daily life better than leather, and a model rated 100 m can handle a swim.

### How often does an automatic need a service?

About every 4 to 5 years. A watchmaker cleans and oils the movement, replaces the seals and adjusts the timing so it keeps good time for years to come. Between services you only need to wipe it with a soft cloth and keep it away from knocks and magnets.
